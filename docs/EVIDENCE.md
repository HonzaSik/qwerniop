# Evidence

What Tenfold's letter combination features rest on, and how far that goes. A feature that affects learning should name its source here, or say that it has none.

## Studies

| Study | Finding | Kind | Used for |
|---|---|---|---|
| DeFulio et al. 2011, *Journal of Applied Behavior Analysis* 44(4), 921–924. [doi:10.1901/jaba.2011.44-921](https://doi.org/10.1901/jaba.2011.44-921). Abstract only. | Novice typists reached fluency in nearly half the time with real words rather than jumbled characters. | Experiment | Combinations will be practised inside real words, never as letter drills (milestone 21). |
| Crump & Logan 2010, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance* 36(3), 662–672. [doi:10.1037/a0018390](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018390) | Skilled typists practised words from one set of letters. Afterwards, trained words were fastest, then new words from the trained letters, then new words from untrained letters, with effects at bigram level too. | Experiment, skilled typists | A pair is a reasonable unit to measure. Within one session, small speed-ups showed at letter and pair level (about 12 to 16 ms for trained pairs, found post hoc), and letter-level transfer faded quickly. Lasting carry-over was not shown. |
| Behmer & Crump 2017, in *Big Data in Cognitive Science*, 319–341. Summary only. | Keystroke times become more sensitive to letter, bigram and trigram frequency with expertise. | Correlational | Pairs are ranked by total time lost, which favours frequent ones. |
| Dhakal et al. 2018, *CHI*. [doi:10.1145/3173574.3174220](https://doi.org/10.1145/3173574.3174220) | In 136 million keystrokes from 168,000 people, times for pairs typed with alternating hands or one hand predict typing speed better than letter repetitions. Hand classes were inferred from the keys, with no finger data and self-selected volunteers, so the study has no same-finger class. | Correlational | Stats show time by kind of move, described as a link and not a cause. |
| Baddeley & Longman 1978, *Ergonomics* 21(8), 627–635 | Training hours for postal workers to learn the keyboard: 34.9 at one hour a day, 49.7 at two 2-hour sessions a day. These are hours of practice, not calendar time; the longer schedule finished in far fewer weeks. | Experiment | Stats show how practice spreads over the last 7 days. |

## What is not established

No trial was found showing that targeting a person's slow letter pairs speeds up learning. The studies above make it plausible, not proven. Pair weighting therefore ships only with a randomized comparison on the learner's own data (ROADMAP, milestone 21), and comes out if that comparison shows nothing.

## Left out

- Rollover (pressing the next key before releasing the last): common and linked to speed in Dhakal et al., but it needs key-release timing near the keystroke path, and nothing found shows that training it helps.
- Letter drills without words: the DeFulio result points against them.
